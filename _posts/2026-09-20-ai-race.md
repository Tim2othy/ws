How does getting richer affect risky decisions in a race?

Read the full paper [here](https://tim2othy.github.io/bachelorarbeit/).

# Intro

Suppose there are two teams, (possibly US and China) competing to build AGI, their have to decide how much to invest in AI development and how much to worry about safety.

One could try to view AI as a purly geopolitical technology.

Similar to nuclear weapons, whoever gets there first controlls the future, can subjugate the other team, can influence economic matters in their own favour.

But there is one major difference between AI and other technologies such as nuclear weapons, AI development might significantly raise economic output, this can have surprising effects on the dynamics of the race.

# Model

To keep the model simple I assume there is only one relevant axis of decision making, I will call this AI intensity. It corresponds both to investing more in AI and in not worrying about safety, in not delaying deployment. I use $a$ for this variable.

We only need three functions to build our model, how economic output and risk relate to AI intensity, and how consumers benefit from economic growth. I choose simple exponential relationships for the first two

$$
c(a) ≡ e^{g a}
$$

$$
S(a) ≡ e^{ - 𝜎 a}.
$$

I call $g$ and $σ$ the growth and hazard parameters respectively.

But I am more interested in the equilibrium that will result in a race. For this we need to specify one last element, how winning the race benefits you. Here the exact details are crutial, for now I will assume winning means you will have more _consumption._ The winner gets full consumption while the loser only gets a fraction 1-b, if b was 0.3 the loser would only get 70% as much consumption as the winner.

Finally to break the perfect symmetry one team starts with an advantage, \Delta the other team has to outspend it on AI by a factor of Delta. If Delta is 10 and the leading team spends 20 on ai the second team would have to spend more than 30
Team 1 will always win the race, and will either play the social optimum, if this deters team 2 from racing, or will use the minimal amount of AI needed to deter team 2.
team 2 will either want to outrace team 1 or accept defeat. In equilibrium team 2 is indifferent between all values.
The intuition for this result as that Team 1 would prefer to choose the social optimum $a^*$.
But this might not deter team 2 from racing, if it does the social optimum is played.
Otherwise, team 1 increases $a_1$ just enough to make team 2 indifferent between racing and accepting defeat.

# Result

While the model is static, a one time decision is made, for intuition we can imagine the model as a dynamic game where decisions are made at each point in time.

We can imagine both teams making a decision each month or year. The leader starts out ahead, and at each point in time both teams increase their AI usage a bit more, the follower to catch up, the leader to stay ahead.
At each point in time both teams consider if it is worth it to further increase $a$, do the benefits:
+ more growth
	+ leading to more utility
+ winning the race
outweigh the negatives
+ more risk

as $a$ grows larger and larger the magnitudes of the these three factors change. Starting with the risk, there are different ways to model this but in the simplest model we choose this always stays the same, each increase in $a$ reduces the likelihood of survival by an equal amount.

The most interesting result is how, if racing makes us wealthier, this makes us more risk averse and in turn less willing to race.


This reflects the core mechanism of my model: the gain from winning over losing shrinks as AI use increases.

Why? More AI use makes everyone wealthier, and wealthier agents care less about additional consumption relative to survival.

It makes sense to race and increase x-risk by 3% if this doubles your consumption from \$1,000 to \$2,000 a year.

But not when your consumption is already \$100,000.
In this way, the dynamics in this race are surprisingly similar to those discussed in #prose[@aschenbrenner2025] and #prose[@jones2025].

As we become wealthier, we're more willing to trade away consumption for security.

While this is an interesting effect, it's unclear whether it's really what would be relevant in a race for AGI.
In practice, I'd expect increasing marginal risk, not decreasing marginal benefit, to be the main driver of deterrence.

Most comparative statics are what one would expect: an increase in enmity makes racing more intense, while a larger gap in capabilities reduces intensity, as shown in @armstrong2013.
More interesting is that higher growth decreases racing intensity.
One might expect that a more valuable post-AGI future makes winning more important, but since a higher growth rate increases both the winners and the losers consumption it creates a wealth effect that makes everyone more risk averse.
An analogy: A person might risk more in a contest to increase his daily income from \$5 to \$10 than from \$50 to \$100.
While the second increase is larger in absolute terms it is less valuable in terms of utility.
This captures the same effect as #prose[@jones2024] and @aschenbrenner2025: the richer we are (or expect to be), the more we value safety over additional consumption.

## Intuition
This effect may help explain why there is less violence today than in previous centuries: the richer we are, the less willing we are to risk everything in a war.

One can imagine this as a general model of tech races, not just limited to AI. If racing also increase GDP/Consumption in doing so it will make people less willing to race, and eventually they will stop once rich enough.

For a potential AI race, the importance of this effect is hard to estimate.

If you trust the other team not to enslave or destroy you, a more glorious shared future should make you less inclined to race.

On the other hand, it's hard to imagine China being persuaded by the US arguing "don't race, there will be so much GDP growth that there's enough for everyone."

The effect of $σ$ also seems relevant: worlds where a technology poses very high risk may actually be safer than intermediate-risk worlds.
This has practical implications increasing public awareness of AI risks could be especially valuable, as a higher intrinsic risk only lowers equilibrium risk if agents are aware of it.
But heterogeneous beliefs complicate matters: if risks are very high, there's an increased chance that some team radically underestimates them and does something foolish @unilateral, but this only strengthens the case for increasing public awareness.

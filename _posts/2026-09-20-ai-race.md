# Might an AI Race be Self-Extinguishing?

*A race that makes the racers richer can end on its own — not because someone wins, and not because it gets too dangerous, but because the prize stops being worth the risk.*

## Two ways to think about an AI race

Read the full paper [here](https://tim2othy.github.io/bachelorarbeit/).
Suppose two teams — say the US and China — are competing to build AGI. Each has to decide how much to invest in AI development and how much to worry about safety.

One way to view AGI is as a purely geopolitical technology. Like nuclear weapons: whoever gets there first controls the future, can subjugate the other side, can shape economic matters in their own favour. On this view an AI race is a classic arms race — racing adds risk, the prize is fixed, and the race ends only when someone wins or the danger becomes unbearable.

But there is one major difference between AI and technologies like nuclear weapons: AI might massively raise economic output. The race itself makes the racers richer. This has a consequence I haven't seen discussed elsewhere: the race can extinguish itself.

## A minimal model

To keep things simple, assume each team has a single dial it can turn, call it **AI intensity**. Turning it up means investing more in AI, worrying less about safety, deploying earlier. Turning it up does two things at once: economic output grows, and the probability of surviving falls.

The third ingredient is standard: **diminishing marginal utility**. Adding more and more consumption raises wellbeing by less and less.

## How much risk is worth taking?

Even with no race at all there's a best setting of the dial — the one that perfectly balances extra growth against extra risk. It has a nice interpretation via the **value of a statistical life year**: how many years' worth of consumption you would give up to gain one year of life. In the US this is currently about 6 (a life year valued around \$240,000, against \$60,000 of annual consumption).

A social planner keeps turning up the dial as long as the growth gained per unit of risk exceeds this value, and stops when the two are equal. The key fact — and the engine of everything below — is that **the value of a life year rises with income**. The richer you are, the less willing you are to risk your life for extra consumption.

## Now make it a race

For a race, winning has to be worth something. I assume the prize is consumption: the winner enjoys full consumption, the loser only a fraction of it — say 30% less. One team starts with a head start, and the other must out-invest it to win.

In equilibrium the leading team always wins. It either plays the planner's optimum, if that's already enough to deter its rival, or escalates just enough that the rival gives up. Although the model is static, you can picture it as escalation over time: each month both teams push a little further — the follower to catch up, the leader to stay ahead.

## Why the race burns itself out

Here is the core dynamic. Every round of escalation makes *both* teams richer — that's the whole point of AI. And the prize for winning is proportional: full consumption rather than 70% of it. In utility terms, that prize is worth roughly the same fixed amount no matter how rich you are. The *cost* of racing, however — the extra chance of extinction — destroys a fixed fraction of *everything you have*. And everything you have keeps growing.

It makes sense to accept an extra 3% risk of extinction to double your consumption from \$1,000 to \$2,000 a year. It doesn't when you're already at \$100,000.

So racing raises the stakes but not the prize. Each step of the race pushes both teams' wealth — and with it the value they place on survival — closer to the point where winning is no longer worth any additional risk. There the follower accepts second place, and the race simply stops. Not because anyone won. Not because racing got more dangerous — in the model, the marginal risk is constant. Because the prize stopped mattering.

The race consumes its own fuel.

## Self-extinguishing is not the same as safe

A race over consumption always ends; the question is how much risk piles up first. In my numerical example with a small head start, the race only dies out after consumption has grown roughly a million-fold — and survival probability has fallen to about 25%, versus 67% under a planner. So the right question isn't whether the race ends, but how early. More trust between the teams and a larger lead for the front-runner both end it earlier.

## The fine print

- **The prize must be riches, not survival.** The mechanism runs on the loser still getting to enjoy most of the new wealth. If losing means being subjugated or destroyed, no amount of wealth extinguishes the race. Whether an AI race burns out depends on what the racers believe they are racing *for*.
- **Faster growth means less racing.** One might expect a more valuable post-AGI future to make winning more important. The opposite happens: higher growth enriches winner and loser alike, and the wealth effect makes both more cautious. A person might risk more to raise their daily income from \$5 to \$10 than from \$50 to \$100 — the second increase is bigger in dollars but worth less in utility.
- **Risk awareness matters.** Worlds where the technology is very dangerous can end up safer than moderately dangerous ones — but only if the racers know the danger. This makes raising public awareness of AI risk directly valuable.
- **Realism.** In an actual AGI race I would expect rising marginal risk, not a shrinking prize, to do most of the deterring. The wealth effect would still operate underneath it.

## A new spin on wealth and peace

That wealth makes us more peaceful is an old idea — it may be part of why there is less violence today than in past centuries — and recent work by Chad Jones and Leopold Aschenbrenner applies it to AI: the richer we are, or expect to be, the more we value safety over further consumption. The twist here is that the *conflict itself* generates the wealth that ends it. A race over riches digs its own grave.

Whether this is reassuring for a real AGI race is another question — it's hard to imagine China being persuaded by the US arguing "don't race, there will be so much GDP growth that there's enough for everyone." But it does point at a cheap lever: anything that credibly makes losing survivable turns a race over survival into a race over riches. And races over riches end on their own.
